Lactic Acidosis: ED Dashboard

An Interactive Clinical Guide for the UK Registrar

Lactic acidosis is not a single disease but a manifestation of profound physiological stress. Understanding its underlying cause is crucial for effective management. It is broadly classified into three main types based on the presence or absence of tissue hypoperfusion.

Type A: Shock

Inadequate Oxygen Delivery

Caused by systemic hypoperfusion where oxygen delivery fails to meet tissue demand. This forces anaerobic metabolism, leading to massive lactate overproduction.

Type B: Metabolic

Cellular Dysfunction

Occurs in haemodynamically stable patients. The primary issue is cellular metabolic dysfunction caused by diseases, drugs, or toxins that impair lactate clearance.

Type D: Gut-Derived

The Neurological Mimic

A rare but critical diagnosis caused by gut bacteria producing D-lactate. Suspect in patients with short-bowel syndrome and unexplained encephalopathy.

Type A: Inadequate Oxygen Delivery

This is the classic shock state, where systemic oxygen delivery fails to meet tissue demand. The resulting hypoxia forces anaerobic metabolism, leading to massive lactate overproduction. Management is focused on aggressively reversing the cause of shock to restore perfusion.

Vicious Cycle:

Shock → Lactate Production → Acidosis → Myocardial Suppression & Vasodilation → Worsening Shock

Type B: Normal Perfusion, Cellular Dysfunction

This type occurs in haemodynamically stable patients where the problem lies in the cell's metabolic machinery, not oxygen delivery. The cause is often a disease, drug, or toxin that poisons mitochondria or excessively accelerates glycolysis. A meticulous history is key.

B1: Underlying Disease

+
  • Liver/Renal Failure: Impaired clearance.
  • Malignancy (Warburg Effect): Massive lactate overproduction (leukaemias/lymphomas).
  • Sepsis: Complex mix of A & B mechanisms.
  • Thiamine (B1) Deficiency: Blocks pyruvate dehydrogenase.
  • DKA, Pheochromocytoma

B2: Drugs & Toxins

+
  • Metformin (MALA): The classic example, risk amplified by AKI.
  • Toxic Alcohols: Ethylene glycol, methanol.
  • Propofol (PRIS), Linezolid, NRTIs: Mitochondrial toxicity.
  • Beta-2 Agonists (Salbutamol): Adrenergic stimulation of glycolysis.
  • Salicylates, Cyanide, Iron

B3: Inborn Errors of Metabolism

+

Rare conditions usually diagnosed in childhood, such as Pyruvate Dehydrogenase Deficiency or mitochondrial myopathies.

Type D: The Neurological Mimic

A rare but critical diagnosis caused by bacterial fermentation of carbohydrates in the gut, leading to absorption of D-lactate. This is easily missed because the standard lab assay only measures L-lactate. Suspect this in a patient with short bowel syndrome presenting with encephalopathy.

Management in the ED is time-critical. It starts with the ABCDE approach and, for septic patients, immediate implementation of the Sepsis Six. Lactate clearance is a key prognostic marker. Specific therapies like bicarbonate, RRT and antidotes are reserved for specific circumstances.

The UK Sepsis Six

A lactate ≥2 mmol/L is a high-risk criterion. This bundle must be delivered within 1 hour.

1. High-Flow Oxygen
2. Blood Cultures
3. IV Antibiotics
4. IV Fluids
5. Measure Lactate
6. Measure Urine Output

Lactate Clearance as a Target

The trend is vital. Aim for >20% clearance at 2 hours. Failure to clear is an ominous sign.

Toxin-Specific Management

  • Metformin (MALA): Primarily supportive care. RRT is the definitive treatment to remove metformin and correct acidosis if severe (pH < 7.1).
  • Toxic Alcohols (Methanol, Ethylene Glycol): Block metabolism with an alcohol dehydrogenase inhibitor (Fomepizole). RRT is often required.
  • Salicylates: Urinary alkalinisation (IV Bicarbonate) to enhance excretion. RRT for severe cases.

Bicarbonate & RRT

These are ICU-level interventions, NOT routine ED therapies.

  • Bicarbonate: Only consider if pH ≤ 7.15 with AKI, in specific poisonings, or for life-threatening hyperkalaemia.
  • Renal Replacement Therapy (RRT): For refractory acidosis, dialysable toxins, or severe fluid/electrolyte derangement.

A summary of high-yield concepts and common pitfalls for the ED registrar.

1. Lactate is a Fuel, Not Just Waste

Think of hyperlactataemia as a sign of significant metabolic stress, not always tissue hypoxia (dysoxia). In sepsis, for example, massive catecholamine release can drive aerobic glycolysis and lactate production even with adequate oxygen delivery. The clinical context is everything.

2. Mind the Albumin

Always calculate the albumin-corrected anion gap. Many critically ill patients are hypoalbuminaemic. A "normal" anion gap of 12 in a patient with an albumin of 25 g/L is actually hiding a significant, unmeasured anion load. This is a commonly missed finding.

3. Use the Delta Ratio to Find the Mixed Picture

A pure high anion gap metabolic acidosis should have a delta ratio of 1-2. A ratio < 1 suggests a concurrent normal anion gap acidosis (e.g., from saline resuscitation). A ratio > 2 suggests a concurrent metabolic alkalosis (e.g., from vomiting).

4. The Trend is Your Friend

A single lactate value is a snapshot; the trend over time tells the story of your resuscitation. Failure to clear lactate by at least 20% within 2 hours is a major red flag indicating persistent shock or an unaddressed underlying cause. Re-evaluate your patient aggressively.

5. Don't Forget D-Lactate

In a patient with a history of short-bowel syndrome or jejunoileal bypass who presents with episodic encephalopathy, ataxia, and slurred speech, think D-lactic acidosis. They will have a high anion gap, but the standard hospital lactate assay (which only measures L-lactate) will be normal. This requires a specific send-out test.
